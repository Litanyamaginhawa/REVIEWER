Week 3 — Ethical Levels of Human Existence

Learning Outcomes

  1. Determine the ethical aspect of human life.
  2. Analyze personal, societal, and environmental ethical issues.
  3. Use ethical reasoning to solve the ethical issues presented.

“Think, Choose, Act!”

  • You made a mistake on a school project. No one noticed. Would you admit your mistake and take responsibility? Why?
  • You see someone being treated unfairly because of their background. What would you do to promote fairness and respect?
  • You notice that your classmates are leaving trash around the school grounds. What would you do to help keep the environment clean?

Main Focus

The module discusses levels of ethical life or moral valuation on a personal, societal, and environmental level. Ethical issues happen in these three levels, so reflection or reasoning should be used to address them carefully. Human persons possess reason and use it to think, analyze, and evaluate ethical issues.

Personal Level

Personal level is about how you treat yourself and others. Treating yourself raises questions about whether suicide, drug abuse, or other actions that harm the self are morally correct. Treating others is in the realm of relationships or behaviors toward others.

  • Do we treat others with respect and dignity or use them as an object of our interest?
  • He/She is able to distinguish between good and evil, right and wrong, moral and immoral.
  • He/She feels within himself/herself an obligation to do what is good and to avoid what is evil.
  • He/She feels accountable for his/her actions, expecting reward or punishments for them.
  • Man is endowed by nature with a moral sense.
  • He/She is self-conscious of his dignity and submits to the duty of doing what is good and avoiding what is wrong.
  • He/She conducts himself/herself according to the “dictates of reason.”

Societal Levels

These are ethical societal concerns that affect society in general.

  • Corruption
  • Poverty
  • Other issues that society faces
  • Issues may happen in the barangay, town, city, province, whole country, or the world in general.

Environmental Levels

The 21st century’s concern is no longer limited to the personal and societal level but includes environmental concerns.

  • Global warming
  • Deforestation
  • Taking care of the environment
  • Taking care of other non-human species

Group Activity — Ethical Decision-Makers

  • Form 7 groups. Each group is given an ethical situation related to the Personal, Societal, or Environmental Level of Human Existence.
  • Prepare and perform a short skit lasting 3–5 minutes.
  • The skit should clearly show the ethical dilemma, choices available, and consequences of the decision.
  • After the skit, one representative explains what ethical level is presented and why.
  • Be creative, realistic, and respectful. All group members should participate.

Rubrics: Understanding of the Ethical Issue — 10; Connection to the Assigned Ethical Level — 10; Content and Message — 10; Creativity and Presentation — 10; Teamwork and Participation — 10; Total — 50 points.

Group Situations in the Module

  • Personal — Academic Honesty: a student can get a higher grade by using unauthorized answers during an examination.
  • Personal — Peer Pressure: friends pressure a student to do something they believe is wrong.
  • Societal — Discrimination: a student is treated unfairly because of their background.
  • Societal — Social Media: a false rumor about a classmate is spreading online; decide whether to share it or stop the spread.
  • Societal — Fairness: students disagree about how limited school resources should be distributed fairly.
  • Environmental — Waste Management: students notice that their school produces too much plastic waste and decide what actions they can take.
  • Environmental — Development vs. Nature: a proposed project will create jobs for the community but may damage the surrounding environment.

Quiz 1 — Important Question

As a college student, how can your everyday decisions demonstrate ethical responsibility at the Personal, Societal, and Environmental levels? Discuss each level and provide at least one concrete example for each.

Criteria: Understanding of the three ethical levels — 10; Quality and relevance of examples — 8; Explanation and ethical reasoning — 7; Organization and clarity — 3; Grammar and mechanics — 2; Total — 30 points.

Week 4 — Sources of Authority in Ethics + Senses of the Self

Sources of Authority

Ethics, as a field of inquiry, seeks to establish principles and guidelines for human behavior. Central to this exploration is the question of where these ethical principles derive their authority. Several sources have historically been cited as the foundation of ethical systems.

Traditional Sources of Authority

  • Law
  • Religion
  • Culture

1. Law

  • Legal systems are a significant source of ethical authority.
  • Laws codify societal norms and values.
  • They provide a framework for regulating behavior and ensuring justice.
  • While laws often reflect ethical principles, they can also conflict with individual or moral beliefs.
  • The law does not tell us what we should do; it works by constraining us from performing acts that we should not do.
  • Examples in the module: no drunk driving; no underage drinking.

2. Religion

  • Religion is a particular system of faith and worship.
  • Often considered a primary source of moral authority.
  • Provides ethical codes and principles derived from sacred texts, divine commandments, or prophetic teachings.
  • Offers a framework for understanding right and wrong, good and evil, based on a belief in a higher power.
  • Divine Command Theory: the belief that something is ethically right if God orders it, and morally bad if God forbids it. One is obliged to obey his/her God in all things.
  • Multiplicity of Religions: each faith places various demands on its followers, resulting in seemingly contradicting ethical standards — “Different religions, different beliefs.”
  • Example in the module: certain religions have prohibitions concerning what food may be consumed while others do not share the same constraints.

3. Culture

  • Culture is the patterns of learned and shared behavior and beliefs of a particular social, ethnic, or age group.
  • Cultural traditions and customs can shape ethical understanding.
  • What is considered right or wrong often varies across cultures, reflecting different values, beliefs, and social practices.

Cultural Relativism

  • What is ethically acceptable or unacceptable is relative or dependent on one’s culture.
  • It teaches tolerance of others from different cultures.
  • We are in no position to judge whether the ethical thought or practice of another culture is acceptable or unacceptable.
  • Cultural relativism suggests morality is relative to a particular culture, while ethical universalism argues for shared ethical principles across cultures.
  • It is a principle that an individual's beliefs and activities should be understood by others in terms of that individual's own culture.
  • No culture is superior to another and moral and ethical systems are relative to cultural context.

Cultural Relativism — Important Examples

  • Arranged marriages: should be understood within the context of a society's traditions, values, and social structures rather than judged against cultures that prioritize individual choice in marriage.
  • Dietary restrictions: the prohibition of pork in Islam and Judaism; understand these dietary laws within the framework of each religion's beliefs, history, and cultural significance.

Benefits of Cultural Relativism

  1. Fosters greater cultural understanding and tolerance: different cultures have their own valid ways of life; individuals can become more open-minded and respectful of cultural diversity.
  2. Helps prevent ethnocentrism: the belief that one's own culture is superior to others; understanding cultural norms and values are relative makes people less likely to impose their own cultural standards on others.
  3. Promotes anthropological and sociological research: researchers can study cultures without bias, leading to more accurate and nuanced understandings of human societies.

Challenges / Criticisms of Cultural Relativism

  • This concept can be seen as endorsing moral relativism.
  • All cultural practices may be deemed equally valid, even those that may violate human rights.
  • Critics argue that this can lead to ethical dilemmas when cultural practices are harmful or oppressive.
  • Cultural relativism can hinder social progress by making it difficult to advocate for changes or improvements within a culture, especially in areas related to gender equality or human rights.
  • It can create challenges in international relations and global governance when applied rigidly, making it difficult to establish common ground or universal standards for issues like human rights, environmental protection, and social justice.

Contemporary Perspectives

  • Reason and Rationality: there is a growing emphasis on reason and rationality as sources of ethical authority. Ethical theories like utilitarianism and deontology rely on logical reasoning and critical thinking to determine right and wrong. These approaches seek to ground morality in objective principles rather than relying solely on tradition or divine command.
  • Individual Conscience: conscience is often described as an inner sense of right and wrong, guiding individual actions and decisions. Its reliability as an ethical compass is debated because it can be influenced by personal biases, upbringing, and social conditioning.
  • Expert Opinion: ethical experts, such as philosophers, ethicists, and theologians, can provide valuable insights and guidance on moral issues. Their expertise can help individuals and societies navigate complex ethical dilemmas. Expert opinions should be critically evaluated and multiple perspectives considered.

Challenges and Considerations

  • These sources of authority are not mutually exclusive and often overlap.
  • The authority of each source can be questioned and challenged.
  • Religious ethics may face criticisms regarding its applicability to diverse societies.
  • Legal systems can be accused of being unjust or outdated.
  • Determining the ultimate source of ethical authority remains a complex and ongoing philosophical inquiry.
  • A critical and reflective approach, considering multiple perspectives and the potential limitations of each source, is essential for developing a robust ethical framework.

Individual Moral Agency

  • Ethics inherently revolves around “the self”—the individual agent who reflects, chooses, and acts.
  • How we perceive our identity and underlying motives shapes our sense of moral duty.

Three Key Senses of Self

  • Subjectivism — personal feeling
  • Psychological Egoism — natural motive
  • Ethical Egoism — rational duty

1. Subjectivism — Moral Authority of the Self

  • The individual human subject is the sole starting point and absolute authority for all moral claims.
  • Core Premise: Moral claims express personal feelings or individual tastes.
  • Key Motto: “What is right for me is right for me; what is right for you is right for you.”
  • Implication: Denies objective or universal moral standards outside sentiment.

Subjectivism — Real-World Case

Digital Piracy & Academic Honesty: A student illegally downloads expensive copyrighted textbooks and software online without paying creators.

  • Subjectivist Defense: The student says software should be free for students and that, because it feels fine, it is not wrong for them.
  • Ethical Flaw: Subjectivism reduces fairness to pure personal preference, making moral dispute resolution impossible.

2. Psychological Egoism — Human Nature

  • A descriptive theory stating that humans are naturally motivated strictly by self-interest in every action.
  • Descriptive Claim: Describes how humans actually behave, not how they ought to act.
  • Hidden Motives: Sacrificial acts are secretly driven by self-serving motives.
  • Primary Drivers: Seeking pride, praise, inner peace, or avoiding guilt and shame.

Psychological Egoism — Real-World Case

The Anonymous Disaster Relief Volunteer: A person spends their weekend volunteering in grueling conditions at a flood shelter and refuses public recognition.

  • Egoist Re-Interpretation: Helping granted a “warm glow” of inner emotional relief.
  • Ethical Critique: Reduces all emotional satisfaction to selfishness, making genuine altruism impossible by definition.

3. Ethical Egoism — Self-Interest as Duty

  • A normative theory stating that individuals ought to pursue their own self-interest as their primary moral duty.
  • Normative Duty: Prescribes how we should act—maximizing long-term flourishing.
  • Rational Egoism: Distinguishes short-sighted greed from long-term self-advancement.
  • Philosophical View: Altruism demands self-sacrifice; self-reliance is the highest virtue.

Ethical Egoism — Real-World Case

Entrepreneurship & Business Growth: A tech founder refuses to surrender company stock to charity out of guilt, choosing to reinvest profits into R&D and company growth.

  • Ethical Egoist Defense: The founder’s highest duty is to build a successful enterprise. Rationally pursuing profit creates jobs and innovation—benefiting society without forced self-sacrifice.

Comparing the Senses of Self

  • Subjectivism: Morality is strictly a matter of individual feeling and opinion. Real-life manifestation: justifying piracy/cheating because “it feels fine to me.” Limitation: eliminates objective standards and prevents resolving disputes.
  • Psychological Egoism: All human actions are secretly motivated by self-interest. Real-life manifestation: interpreting charity work as seeking an internal “warm glow.” Limitation: circular logic; cannot prescribe how we ought to act.
  • Ethical Egoism: Individuals have a moral duty to maximize long-term self-interest. Real-life manifestation: prioritizing personal career and financial growth over self-sacrifice. Limitation: fails to resolve conflicting interests fairly when harm occurs.

Beyond Egoism — Developing Moral Maturity

  • Recognizing Genuine Altruism: Humans can care for others’ well-being for their own sake, beyond personal benefit.
  • Balancing Self & Others: Moral maturity requires honoring personal autonomy while respecting universal rights and social duties.
  • Shared Rational Dialogue: Moving past subjective opinion toward reasoned ethical discussion that fosters social justice.

Week 5 — Ethical Levels of Moral Valuation, Moral Reasoning & Social Responsibility

Ethical Levels of Moral Valuation

Different levels at which human actions, decisions, and behaviors are judged as morally right or wrong. Moral valuation helps people determine whether an act is good, bad, acceptable, or unacceptable according to ethical principles.

1. Pre-Moral (Non-Moral) Level

  • Actions are evaluated based on personal needs, desires, pleasure, or pain rather than moral principles.
  • Focuses on self-interest.
  • Decisions are based on rewards or avoidance of punishment.
  • Common among young children.
  • Examples: a child obeys parents because of a reward or to avoid punishment; a student studies only to avoid failing grades.

2. Moral Level

  • A person recognizes moral rules, duties, and responsibilities.
  • Actions are judged according to what is right and wrong.
  • Considers fairness, justice, and respect for others.
  • Follows ethical principles and social norms.
  • Acknowledges personal responsibility.
  • Examples: returning a lost wallet because it is the right thing to do even when no one is watching; following traffic rules even when no police officers are present.

3. Ethical Level

  • The highest level of moral valuation.
  • Individuals critically examine the reasons behind moral rules.
  • Decisions are based on universal ethical principles and human values.
  • Uses reason and critical thinking.
  • Reflects on the consequences of actions.
  • Considers human dignity, rights, and the common good.
  • Examples: a whistleblower exposes corruption despite personal risks because public welfare is more important than personal gain; a doctor treats all patients equally regardless of social status.

Moral Reasoning

The process of thinking carefully about what is right and wrong when making decisions. It involves using ethical principles, values, and judgment to determine the best course of action.

  1. Recognition of a Moral Issue: identify that a situation involves an ethical concern. Example: discovering that a friend is cheating on an examination.
  2. Gathering Facts: know the facts before making judgments — What happened? Who is affected? What are the consequences? Are there rules or policies involved? Example: Did the friend intentionally cheat or make an honest mistake?
  3. Evaluating Alternatives: different actions may be possible — ignore the cheating, talk to the friend privately, or report the incident. Each option has moral implications.
  4. Applying Ethical Principles: honesty, justice, responsibility, respect, and fairness help determine the best action.
  5. Making a Moral Judgment: after evaluating the situation, decide what is morally right.

Social Responsibility

The obligation of individuals to act for the benefit of society and contribute to the welfare of others. It means considering how one’s actions affect other people and the community.

  1. Concern for Others: cares about the welfare of others. Example: helping victims during natural disasters.
  2. Accountability: being accountable for one’s actions. Example: a student admits a mistake rather than blaming others.
  3. Respect for Human Rights: respecting the dignity and rights of every person. Example: treating people equally regardless of religion, gender, or social status.
  4. Environmental Responsibility: protecting natural resources and the environment. Example: proper waste disposal and recycling.
  5. Community Participation: active involvement in improving society. Example: joining outreach programs and community projects.

Week 6 — Moral Agent

Examples of Moral Communities

  • Family: members are expected to care for, respect, and support one another. Parents have a moral responsibility to provide for their children.
  • School Community: students, teachers, and staff share ethical responsibilities such as honesty, fairness, and respect. Students should not cheat because it harms trust within the community.
  • Workplace: employers and employees have duties to treat each other fairly and professionally. Employers should provide safe working conditions.
  • Nation or Society: citizens are part of a larger moral community where laws and ethical principles guide behavior. People are expected to respect others’ rights and follow just laws.
  • Religious Community: members follow shared moral values and responsibilities. Example: helping the poor and practicing compassion.
  • Global Human Community: all human beings are considered members of a worldwide moral community. Example: respecting human rights regardless of nationality, race, or religion.
  • Animals: many ethicists argue animals deserve moral consideration because they can feel pain and suffering. Example: avoiding cruelty to animals. The module cites Republic Act No. 8485 or Animal Welfare Act of 1998.
  • The Environment and Future Generations: environmental ethics expands the moral community to include ecosystems and people who will live in the future. Example: conserving natural resources and reducing pollution.

Moral Agent: Characteristics

A moral agent possesses qualities that enable ethical decision-making and responsible behavior.

  1. Rationality: ability to think critically and evaluate situations before acting. Example: verifying information before sharing it online.
  2. Free Will: capacity to choose among different courses of action. Example: choosing to study honestly or cheat during an examination.
  3. Moral Awareness: ability to recognize that a situation involves ethical considerations. Example: recognizing that taking office supplies for personal use is unethical.
  4. Conscience: inner sense that guides a person toward what is right and warns against wrongdoing. Example: feeling guilty after lying and deciding to tell the truth.
  5. Responsibility: willingness to accept the consequences of one’s actions. Example: admitting a mistake in a group project and helping correct it.
  6. Empathy: ability to understand and share the feelings of others. Example: supporting a classmate who is being bullied.
  7. Integrity: consistency between one’s values, words, and actions. Example: refusing to accept a bribe even when no one is watching.

Moral Accountability

Moral accountability refers to the responsibility of individuals for their actions, decisions, and consequences. Since moral agents are capable of making choices, they are answerable for the outcomes of those choices.

Conditions for Moral Accountability

  1. Knowledge: a person must understand what they are doing and recognize its moral implications.
  2. Freedom: the action must be performed voluntarily and not entirely under force or coercion.
  3. Intentionality: the action must be done deliberately rather than accidentally.
  4. Control: the individual must have sufficient control over the action.

Importance of Moral Accountability

  1. Promotes Responsible Behavior: knowing that actions have consequences encourages ethical decision-making.
  2. Builds Trust: accountability fosters trust in relationships, schools, workplaces, and communities.
  3. Strengthens Character: accepting responsibility for mistakes helps individuals grow morally and emotionally.
  4. Supports Justice: people who cause harm should answer for their actions, while those who act ethically deserve recognition.

Carl Rogers

According to Carl Rogers, a fully functioning person:

  • Understands and accepts their true self.
  • Is aware of their feelings and emotions.
  • Makes decisions based on genuine values and experiences.
  • Continuously grows and develops as a person.
  • Is open to new experiences and learning.

Abraham Maslow

  • An American psychologist.
  • Best known for developing the Hierarchy of Needs, a theory that explains what motivates human behavior.
  • Considered one of the founders of Humanistic Psychology.
  • Humanistic Psychology focuses on human potential, personal growth, and self-fulfillment.

Moral Rights, Duties, and Responsibility

  • “To be a person is to be a bearer of rights.”
  • Moral rights refer to the moral rights of humans and are identified with human rights.
  • Rights correlate with duties; protecting and promoting other persons’ rights requires duties and responsibilities.
  • Only moral agents have both rights and duties.
  • Only a moral agent can be responsible and accountable to his/her actions.
  • Accountability is a natural product of rationality, which consists of reason (or intelligence) and free will (or freedom).
  • The will is the power of the soul by which a human being is in control of actions.

Kohlberg — Separate

Lawrence Kohlberg

Lawrence Kohlberg (1927–1987), American psychologist. The module presents six stages of moral development under three levels.

Level 1 — Pre-Conventional Morality

Stage 1 — Obedience and Punishment Orientation:

  • Powerful authorities hand down a fixed set of rules.
  • The rules must be unquestioningly obeyed.
  • Stealing is bad because you will get punished.

Stage 2 — Instrumental-Relativist:

  • There is not just one right view handed down by authorities.
  • Different individuals have different viewpoints.
  • Each person is free to pursue his or her individual interests.

Stage Examples

  • Stage 1 example: Jairo leaves a classmate’s pencil case alone because he remembers the teacher’s punishment for taking things that do not belong to him.
  • Stage 2 example: A student studies hard because parents promised a new toy for a high grade; the behavior is driven by personal reward.

Level 2 — Conventional Morality

Stage 3 — Good Interpersonal Relationships:

  • People should live up to the expectations of family and community.
  • Behave in “good” ways.
  • Good motives and interpersonal feelings such as love, empathy, trust, and concern for others matter.

Stage 4 — Maintaining the Social Order:

  • Emphasis on obeying laws, respecting authority, and performing duties.
  • Social order should be maintained.
  • Decisions are made from the perspective of society as a whole.

Stage 3 and Stage 4 Examples

  • Stage 3 example: Randy shares part of his meal with Villa because he cares about his friend and believes good friends should help one another.
  • Stage 4 example: A citizen pays taxes honestly even when there is an opportunity to avoid doing so because paying taxes supports public services and society’s welfare.

Level 3 — Post-Conventional Morality

Stage 5 — Social Contract Orientation:

  • Considers what makes a good society.
  • Considers the rights and values a society ought to uphold.
  • Existing societies are evaluated using these considerations.

Stage 6 — Universal Human Ethics:

  • Principles of justice require treating claims of all parties impartially.
  • Respect the basic dignity of all people.

Stage 5 and Stage 6 Examples

  • Stage 5 example: A student respectfully advocates changing a school rule that unfairly prevents some students from joining activities so all students have equal opportunities.
  • Stage 6 example: A witness testifies in court to protect an innocent person even though powerful people threaten punishment, believing honesty and justice must be upheld regardless of personal consequences.

Heinz Dilemma

A woman is dying and a drug may save her. The druggist charges $2,000 for a small dose that cost $200 to produce. Heinz can raise only $1,000, asks for a cheaper price or payment later, but the druggist refuses. Heinz breaks into the laboratory to steal the drug for his wife.

Question: Should Heinz have broken into the laboratory to steal the drug for his wife? Why or why not?

  • Pre-Conventional — Obedience: arguments may focus on punishment or prison.
  • Pre-Conventional — Self-interest: arguments may focus on being happier if the wife is saved despite consequences.
  • Conventional — Conformity: arguments may focus on being a good husband and the wife’s expectations.
  • Conventional — Law-and-order: arguments may focus on the law prohibiting stealing and accepting prescribed punishment.
  • Post-Conventional — Social contract orientation: arguments may focus on the right to choose life and the scientist’s right to fair compensation.
  • Post-Conventional — Universal human ethics: arguments may focus on saving human life as a more fundamental value than property rights.
